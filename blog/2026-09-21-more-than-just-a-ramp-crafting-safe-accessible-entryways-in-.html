More Than Just a Ramp: Crafting Safe & Accessible Entryways in Norfolk, NE Homes

It's September here in Norfolk, NE, and that crisp morning air reminds us that the seasons are turning. For many of us, it means pulling out a favorite sweater and enjoying the beautiful fall colors. But for others, the changing weather – and the very design of their homes – presents a constant challenge. I often meet folks whose loved ones rely on a wheelchair, and the conversation almost always turns to the front door. "Justin," they'll ask, "how can we make getting in and out of our house safe? It feels like every step is a hazard."

I've been a contractor for years, and my team and I at Watts ATP Contractor have seen firsthand the difference a properly designed entryway can make. It’s not just about adding a ramp; it’s about creating true freedom and safety. Many homes in Northeast Nebraska, especially older builds, simply weren't designed with wheelchair accessibility in mind. Those two or three steps up to the front door, a narrow hallway, or a tricky threshold can become a daily barrier, threatening independence and even leading to falls. My goal today is to walk you through some key considerations for creating an entryway that's not just accessible, but genuinely safe and welcoming, no matter the season.

Beyond the Ramp: Essential Considerations for Entryway Safety

When most people think about wheelchair accessibility, their minds jump straight to ramps. And yes, a well-built ramp is fundamental. But true entryway safety encompasses so much more. In my experience, a holistic approach is crucial.

First, let's talk about the ramp itself. The slope is paramount. ADA guidelines recommend a 1:12 slope, meaning for every inch of rise, you need 12 inches of run. So, if your entryway has a 24-inch rise, you'll need a 24-foot ramp. Trying to make a ramp too steep to save space is a recipe for disaster, especially in our harsh Nebraska winters when ice and snow are factors. A gentle slope allows for easier ascent and descent, reducing strain on the user and their caregiver.

Next, consider the materials and surface. Here in Norfolk, NE, we experience everything from scorching summer sun to freezing blizzards. This means your ramp material needs to be durable and, most importantly, provide excellent traction. While treated lumber is common, I often recommend composite decking or specialized non-slip surfaces. These materials resist warping, splintering, and, crucially, offer better grip when wet or frosty. Avoid smooth, painted surfaces. We also ensure that ramps are wide enough – at least 36 inches clear width – to accommodate wheelchairs comfortably, with proper edge protection to prevent wheels from slipping off. If you're looking into specific ramp solutions, you can learn more about our wheelchair ramp installation services.

Beyond the ramp, doorways and thresholds are often overlooked bottlenecks. Many older homes in our service area have standard 28-30 inch doorways, which are simply too narrow for most wheelchairs. Widening a doorway to at least 32-36 inches is a common, yet critical, modification. This often involves adjusting door frames and sometimes even relocating light switches or outlets. Equally important are thresholds. A raised threshold, even a small one, can be a major obstacle. We aim for zero-threshold entries where possible
